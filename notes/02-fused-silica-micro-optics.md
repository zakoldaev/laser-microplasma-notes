# Fused-silica surface relief and micro-optics

> **Summary:** This note summarizes the development of laser microplasma processing from controlled relief formation on fused silica to functional micro-optical elements.

The work followed the porous-glass studies and focused on whether the same glass–graphite geometry could be used to produce reproducible surface relief on dense fused silica.

## Processing fused silica

A polished fused-silica plate was placed in contact with pressed graphite. A ytterbium-fiber laser at about **1.064 µm** passed through the glass and was focused at the glass–graphite interface. Pulse durations from **50 to 200 ns** and repetition rates from **20 to 100 kHz** were investigated. Beam scanning was performed with a two-axis galvanometric system and focusing optics with a focal length of about **210 mm** [1,2].

For fused silica, smooth structures without obvious cracks or chips were obtained around **15 W average power** and a scan speed of about **300 mm/s**. The same conditions were less suitable for some other glasses, including BK7 and US-1, where cracking or local damage was more common [2].

Average power, pulse duration and number of passes were varied to control the surface relief. Across the investigated conditions, the relief depth ranged from about **0.2 to 5 µm** and changed approximately linearly with laser power over the tested ranges [2].

A structured area of about **10 × 10 mm²** could typically be produced in roughly **5–15 min**, depending on the geometry and spacing of the written elements. Graphite deposited during processing was removed by wet laser cleaning in distilled water [1].

## Microlens arrays

Microlens arrays were fabricated with lens diameters from about **50 to 500 µm** and periods from **10 to 1000 µm**. Typical relief heights were about **1–2 µm**. The fabricated profiles were not ideal spherical caps and often had a comparatively flat central region [3].

Arrays with lens diameters of **150, 200 and 400 µm** were characterized in more detail. Measured focal lengths were about **30–35 mm**, with numerical apertures of **0.0025–0.0057**. The smallest measured focal spot was approximately **90 ± 10 µm** [3].

For laser processing, a microscope objective was placed after the microlens array to reduce the spot size in the processing plane. In this configuration, arrays of holes of about **10 µm** were produced in a **60 nm titanium film**.

The lens profile could be modified by post-processing. Chemical smoothing in hydrofluoric acid for about **15–30 min** rounded the initially flat-topped profile. A later study examined CO₂-laser smoothing as an alternative approach [4].

## Random phase plates

Random phase plates were fabricated as square cells with two phase levels, **0** and **π**. The required relief was estimated from `hπ = λ / [2(n − 1)]`.

Cell sizes from **250 to 680 µm** were tested. With a **10 mm** beam and a **190 mm** collective lens, the **500 µm** plate produced the most uniform output in the initial experiment. When the concept was tested with a more coherent **532 nm picosecond laser**, interference fringes became stronger and the best result shifted to the **250 µm** plate.

This showed that beam homogenization depended on both the written phase pattern and the coherence of the incident beam. Reported optical losses were about **6.5–9.4%** [1,6].

## Diffraction phase gratings

Diffraction gratings were fabricated with periods from about **55 to 400 µm** and relief depths from about **0.2 to 1.3 µm** [5].

One grating studied in detail had a period of **100 µm**, a modified-width-to-period ratio of about **0.5**, and a relief depth near **0.5 µm**. The profile was smooth rather than an ideal binary trench. Roughness in the region most affected by the plasma was about **0.02–0.03 µm**, while the center of the written line remained close to the roughness of the original fused-silica surface.

The measured diffraction pattern was compared with calculation. The grating was also used for simultaneous processing of a **20 nm titanium film**. Depending on the patterned area, fabrication and cleaning took roughly **1–10 min** [5].

The same surface-relief approach was later extended to multilevel phase plates and other phase structures [6].

## What was established

These studies showed that the glass–graphite process could be used for controllable micrometre-scale relief formation on fused silica over centimetre-scale areas. The investigated relief range was approximately **0.2–5 µm**.

The same fabrication approach was used to produce and test microlens arrays, random phase plates and diffraction gratings. The experiments also identified practical limitations, including non-ideal microlens profiles, coherence-dependent performance of random phase plates and finite surface roughness in the written structures.
