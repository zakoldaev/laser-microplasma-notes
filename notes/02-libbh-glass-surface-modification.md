# Behind the experiment 02 — LIBBH as a method for glass surface modification

## Why this note matters

This paper is an important bridge in the development of the research.

The earlier porous-glass work focused on laser-induced densification as a way to passivate porous glass and protect it from environmental aging.

This 2015 paper formulated the broader processing concept explicitly as:

**Laser-induced black-body heating (LIBBH)**

and showed that the same physical idea could be used not only for porous-glass densification, but also for deliberate surface modification and fabrication of micro-optical structures on quartz glass.

## Publication

R. A. Zakoldaev, M. M. Sergeev, G. K. Kostyuk, V. P. Veiko

## Core idea of LIBBH

The method uses an absorbing graphite plate placed in direct contact with the backside of a transparent glass sample.

The incident laser radiation passes through the transparent glass and is strongly absorbed by the graphite.

The graphite then acts as a localized high-temperature source near the glass surface.

This makes it possible to process glass even when the glass itself has low absorption at the laser wavelength.

The key conceptual chain is:

laser radiation  
→ transmission through transparent glass  
→ absorption by graphite  
→ localized high-temperature region  
→ glass heating and modification

## Why the name “black-body heating”?

The practical idea was to separate the laser-absorption step from the transparent material being processed.

Instead of requiring the glass itself to absorb the laser efficiently, an external absorber converts the laser energy into heat very close to the glass surface.

This broadens the range of laser wavelengths and pulse durations that can potentially be used for transparent-material processing.

## Published scope

The paper reports two main applications of the same method:

1. surface densification of porous glass;
2. creation of micro-optical elements on quartz glass.

This is what makes the paper important in the chronology of the project.

The research direction was no longer only:

> How can porous glass be stabilized?

It became:

> Can the same indirect laser-heating concept become a general tool for shaping and modifying transparent optical materials?

## Published processing concept

The paper used a pulsed near-infrared fiber laser.

Published qualitative elements of the setup include:

| Element | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Transparent glass sample | Material to be modified |
| Graphite plate | Strong absorber / local heater |
| Pulsed NIR fiber laser | Energy source |
| Direct glass–graphite contact | Creates localized heating near the rear glass surface |
| Scanning beam | Enables spatially controlled modification |

Exact process parameters for individual structures should be reconstructed from the paper, original laboratory files, and conference material rather than from memory alone.

## Connection to the earlier porous-glass work

The first note in this archive documents laser passivation of porous glass.

That experiment already established the usefulness of localized thermal densification.

This paper broadened the idea by placing both porous-glass densification and micro-optical fabrication under one processing framework: LIBBH.

The progression was therefore approximately:

porous-glass aging problem  
→ localized surface densification  
→ LIBBH as a general processing concept  
→ controlled surface microstructuring  
→ micro-optical elements  
→ later laser-microplasma studies

## Why this paper is useful for the archive

The paper is especially useful because it captures the moment when the research moved from solving one materials problem to defining a reusable fabrication method.

For somebody entering the field today, the most useful questions are not only what structures were fabricated, but:

- Why was graphite chosen?
- How critical was direct contact?
- How was the focal position adjusted?
- What did the heated region or plasma actually look like?
- How repeatable was the process?
- How quickly did graphite contaminate the glass?
- Which glass types worked best?
- Which regimes caused melting, roughness, cracking, or unstable relief?
- What was learned at the conference stage before the journal paper was finalized?

These are exactly the details that can be added later from original material.

## Connection to the next stage

The LIBBH concept was subsequently developed into more detailed glass-microstructuring studies, including:

G. K. Kostyuk, M. M. Sergeev, R. A. Zakoldaev, E. B. Yakovlev  
“Fast microstructuring of silica glasses surface by NIR laser radiation”  
*Optics and Lasers in Engineering* 68, 16–24 (2015).  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.optlaseng.2014.12.004

That work demonstrated phase diffraction gratings and random phase plates and investigated the technology using several glass compositions.

Later studies moved toward phase gratings, multilevel phase structures, crystalline materials, and a more explicit treatment of laser microplasma and focusing conditions.
